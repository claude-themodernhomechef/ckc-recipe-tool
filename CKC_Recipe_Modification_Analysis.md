## 2. Your Description Style

Your menu descriptions follow a distinct and recognizable pattern. Here is what makes them yours:

**Structure:** They are almost always one sentence, occasionally two. They flow as a continuous thought rather than a list. You describe the dish as a journey through flavors and textures, leading the reader from the protein or hero ingredient through the technique, then the accompaniments, and ending with the sensory payoff.

**Vocabulary:** You favor specific, evocative words: "velvety," "silky," "bright," "vibrant," "layers of complexity," "warming," "melt in your mouth," "bold," "comforting," "rustic," "depth," "balance." You pair opposing descriptors to create tension: "hearty yet fresh," "rich without the heaviness," "sweet and savory."

**What you never do:** You never list ingredients with commas the way a food blogger would (except for the Minestrone, Row 289, which reads more like a shorthand note than a published description). You never use technical cooking terms without context. You never lead with the protein alone.

**Pattern examples:**

- Row 290 (Salmon): "a bright and zesty melt in your mouth salmon over protein-rich mashed potatoes to absorb the juices" -- Starts with the sensory experience, names the protein mid-sentence, ends with the functional pairing.
- Row 337 (Chuck Roast): "Tender chuck roast slow-braised to perfection, served with rich au jus over velvety dairy-free mashed sweet potatoes and accompanied by honey-glazed carrots for a balanced blend of savory depth and natural sweetness." -- Technique first, then layered sides, ending with the flavor promise.
- Row 351 (Chicken Fricassee): "tender, braised chicken in a silky, herb-infused sauce, served over buttery parmesan orzo to soak up every last drop of flavor." -- Lowercase, intimate, ends with an action that makes the reader imagine eating it.

**Key insight:** Your descriptions consistently sell the entire plate, not just the main. You describe sides and accompaniments as part of the experience. This is a private chef's instinct. You are not writing a recipe title. You are writing a menu card.

## 3. Low-FODMAP: The Most Nuanced Protocol (52 Modifications)

Low-FODMAP is by far the most frequently modified protocol, and it reveals the most about your clinical nutrition knowledge. The modifications follow a strict internal logic:

### The Garlic-Infused Oil Rule

This is your signature move. In **38 out of 52 LF modifications**, the primary action is removing garlic and/or onion and replacing them with garlic-infused oil. This works because FODMAPs (specifically fructans) are water-soluble but not fat-soluble. When garlic is infused in oil, the flavor compounds transfer to the fat while the problematic fructans stay in the discarded garlic solids.

This is not common knowledge. Most food bloggers would simply say "omit garlic." You know that removing garlic without replacing the flavor collapses the dish, so you specify the exact swap and often quantify it:

- "Replace garlic and 2 tbsp of oil with garlic-infused oil" (Row 300, Cilantro Lime Rice)
- "Replace garlic and oil with 1 tbsp garlic-infused oil" (Row 289, Summer Minestrone)
- "Replace 3 tbsp of olive oil with garlic-infused oil" (Row 290, Salmon with Salsa Verde)

You are not just removing the FODMAP. You are preserving the garlic flavor through a fat-soluble workaround.

### The Scallion Green Rule

When onion needs removal, you frequently specify using only the green parts of scallions as a replacement:

- "Only use the green part from scallions" (Row 300, Cilantro Lime Rice)
- "Replace shallots with greens from 2 scallions" (Row 308, Moroccan Chicken Meatballs)
- "Only use the scallion greens" (Row 312, Ginger Soy Herb Salad)
- "Replace onion with green onion tops" (Row 357, Coconut Shrimp Bowl)

This is another clinical move. The white and light green parts of scallions contain fructans; the dark green tops do not. You know exactly where the FODMAP cutoff is on the allium.

### Legume and Bean Removal

Beans and legumes are high-FODMAP due to GOS (galacto-oligosaccharides). When they appear in a recipe, you remove them but only when they are a supporting ingredient, not the star:

- "Remove cannellini beans" (Row 289, Minestrone, where beans are one of many components)
- "Remove chickpeas and medjool dates" (Row 326, Spiced Roasted Squash, where the squash is the star)
- "Remove peanut butter" (Row 323, Coconut Chicken, since peanuts are a legume with FODMAP concerns)

You never attempt to make a recipe like Chickpea Soup (Row 315) Low-FODMAP because the legume IS the recipe.

### When You Don't Modify for LF

Notably, recipes like Black Rice Pilaf (Row 293) get the simplest possible note: "Follow the recipe for simple black rice." This reveals a critical decision pattern. When a recipe has a complex version and a simple version, and the simple version is already compliant, you reference it rather than rewriting the complex one. You do not over-engineer.

Recipes with no LF modification tend to be ones where the FODMAP-containing ingredients are so central that removing them would create a different dish entirely.

## 4. Dairy-Free: Strategic Swaps by Category (33 Modifications)

Your DF modifications reveal a clear taxonomy of dairy replacements organized by the functional role of the dairy in the recipe:

### Cream/Richness Function -> Coconut Milk

When dairy serves as the richness or body of a sauce, soup, or braise, you consistently reach for coconut milk:

- "Replace heavy cream with coconut milk" (Row 299, Chicken Tikka Masala)
- "Replace heavy cream with coconut milk and parmesan with a DF parmesan" (Row 333, Marry Me Chicken)
- "Replace half and half with coconut milk" (Row 343, Creamy Chicken Stew)
- "Replace heavy cream with a 14 oz can of coconut milk" (Row 376, Chicken Florentine Soup)

### Tangy/Cultured Function -> Coconut Yogurt

When the dairy is yogurt or sour cream providing tang and acidity, you swap to plain unsweetened coconut yogurt:

- "Replace greek yogurt with plain coconut yogurt" (Row 324, Cucumber Salad)
- "Replace greek yogurt with unsweetened coconut yogurt" (Rows 357, 358, 359, 375 -- consistent across shrimp bowls, salmon, chicken skewers, and cauliflower)

### Cheese/Umami Function -> Nutritional Yeast or Specific Brands

When cheese is providing umami and saltiness, you use nutritional yeast, sometimes combined with miso paste or porcini mushroom powder:

- "Replace parmesan cheese with nutritional yeast and 1 tbsp miso paste or porcini mushroom powder" (Row 289, Minestrone)
- "Remove all the cheeses and replace with 1/4-1/2 c nutritional yeast" (Row 327, Potato Cauliflower Soup)

When cheese needs to melt or have texture, you call out specific brands:

- "Replace with Follow Your Heart brand vegan parmesan" (Rows 321, 322)
- "Replace cottage cheese and mozzarella with vegan ricotta and mozzarella. We like the Kite Hill brand" (Row 363, Turkey Zucchini Lasagna)

### Butter Function -> DF Butter or Olive Oil

- "Replace butter with olive oil" (Row 381, Dijon Beef Stew, where butter is a cooking fat)
- "Replace butter with DF butter" (in mashed potato recipes, where butter is a finishing agent)

### The Key Insight

You never make a blanket "use DF alternative" statement when the dairy plays a critical flavor role. You specify the exact swap because you know that coconut milk behaves differently than oat milk in a braise, and that nutritional yeast gives the savory depth that a generic "DF cheese" might not. The only time you write "DF alternative" generically is when the dairy's role is structural (like milk in meatball mixture) rather than flavor-forward.

## 5. Gluten-Free: Precision Thickener Swaps (29 Modifications)

Your GF modifications fall into distinct categories based on where gluten appears:

### Thickener Replacement: Flour -> Arrowroot or GF 1:1

This is where your specificity shines. You choose between arrowroot powder and 1:1 GF flour based on the application:

**Arrowroot for gravies and thin sauces:**
- "Replace flour with 2 tbsp arrowroot powder to thicken the gravy" (Row 286, Lamb)
- "Replace 1/4 c flour with 1 tbsp arrowroot starch" (Row 384, Lamb Shank)

You know that arrowroot creates a clearer, more glossy sauce than GF flour blends, making it ideal for au jus and braising liquids. You also reduce the quantity (1/4 cup flour becomes 1 tbsp arrowroot) because arrowroot is a more efficient thickener.

**1:1 GF flour for structural applications:**
- "Replace all-purpose flour with 1:1 GF alternative" (Rows 292, 298, 302, 333, 353, 354, 355, 371, 376)

When flour is coating chicken, binding meatballs, or making dumplings, you use 1:1 GF flour because the recipe needs the structural properties that arrowroot alone cannot provide.

### Pasta and Grain Swaps

- "Replace pasta with gf alternative" (Row 289, Minestrone)
- "We like brown rice pasta for the most comparable texture" (Row 335, Italian Meatballs)
- "Use GF orzo such as cassava flour orzo" (Row 352, Parmesan Orzo)
- "Replace couscous with GF couscous or cauliflower rice" (Row 361)

You specify the variety when texture matters. Brown rice pasta for Italian dishes because it holds up closest to semolina. Cassava flour orzo because it mimics the density of wheat orzo.

### Soy Sauce -> Tamari

- "Replace soy sauce with tamari" (Rows 312, 364, 365)

Applied selectively. Only when soy sauce actually appears in the recipe.

### Breadcrumb Swaps

- "Use GF panko for the recipe" (Row 308)
- "Replace bread and breadcrumbs with GF panko" (Row 321)
- "Remove croutons" (Rows 322, 345 -- when the breadcrumb element is a topping, not structural, you simply remove rather than replace)

## 10. When You Choose NOT to Modify

The absence of a modification is as telling as its presence:

- **Already highly compliant (no mods needed):** Roasted Sweet Potatoes (7 native tags), Steamed Rice (7 tags), Thai Sticky Rice (6 tags)

## 13. New Low-FODMAP Patterns: Advanced Techniques

The garlic-infused oil and scallion green rules carry forward perfectly. But batch 2 introduces several new techniques:

### The "Cook and Discard" Method

This is new and clinically sophisticated:

- Row 398 (Tender Brisket): "Ok to consume the beef as is, discard the onion, carrot and parsnip once cooked. The vegetables will impart flavor without the FODMAPs."
- Row 407 (Thai Short Ribs): "Ok to use shallots but discard once finished cooking."

This leverages the same principle as garlic-infused oil but applied to whole aromatics in braises. FODMAPs are water-soluble, so they leach into the braising liquid at lower concentrations than if the vegetables were consumed. By discarding the solids, you keep the flavor contribution while reducing FODMAP load. This is a technique you would only see from someone who understands the biochemistry, not just the food list.

### Specific LF Sauce Replacements

- Row 488 (Chicken Tortilla Soup): "Use LF enchilada sauce."
- Row 493 (Pomegranate Pot Roast): "Replace onion soup mix with chicken bouillon."

You know that commercial sauces and seasoning mixes contain hidden garlic and onion powder. Rather than saying "check the label," you specify exact replacements. This saves the cook from the frustrating label-reading loop.

### Fennel Flagged for LF

- Row 426 (Halibut in Papillote): "Only use the green tops from the leeks. Remove fennel."

Fennel is moderate-to-high FODMAP due to fructan content. This is a nuanced call. Most people would only flag garlic and onion. You know fennel is in the same FODMAP family.

### Corn Removal for LF

- Row 415 (Shrimp Lettuce Cups): "Remove corn."
- Row 449 (Shrimp Taco Salad): "Remove corn."
- Row 481 (Grilled Flank Steak): "Remove corn."

Corn contains sorbitol and is a moderate FODMAP in larger servings. You consistently remove it from LF modifications, which is a detail that many LF guides miss or treat as "low in small servings."

## 20. Menu Description Evolution: Batch 2 Shows Refinement

The descriptions in batch 2 are longer, more vivid, and more structurally complex than batch 1. Some patterns:

**Cooking technique opens more descriptions:** "Char-grilled," "Pan-seared," "Slow-braised," "Oven-roasted" appear as first words more often. This gives the reader a mental image of the kitchen before they taste the dish.

**Specific ingredient callouts increase:** "golden saffron tahdig rice," "gingery bok choy sauteed in fresh ginger," "crispy sage, fennel seed, and toasted panko." These are not generic. They name the exact garnish or technique that makes the dish special.

**The "finished with" construction becomes signature:** "finished with a vibrant basil-spinach Green Goddess dressing," "finished with melty jack cheese and bright cilantro," "finished with golden seared tofu, lime, and fresh herbs." This word signals the final flourish. It is the private chef's equivalent of "plated and garnished."

**New vocabulary additions:** "punchy" (Row 420, 440), "blistered" (Row 484), "char-grilled" (Row 490, 492), "silky" (Row 453), "velvety" (Rows 396, 416). Your sensory lexicon is expanding.
